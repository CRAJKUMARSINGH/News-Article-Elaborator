Electronic Measurement Books in PWD: Abstract Entries Masking Line-by-Line Reality, Fueling Corruption Risks
In the Public Works Department (PWD), the shift to Electronic Measurement Books (e-MB) was meant to bring transparency, accuracy, and accountability to civil works billing. Instead, a growing body of evidence suggests that the current system often delivers the opposite: a sanitised abstract that conceals the very details needed to detect over-measurement, quality shortfalls, and one of the most persistent sources of corruption in road construction — manipulated bitumen records.
The Core Problem: Abstract Without Substance
The electronic Measurement Book currently in use is, in practice, little more than a summary abstract. It does not capture line-by-line measurements as traditionally recorded in physical Measurement Books. Critical details — the exact chainage-wise quantities, layer-wise thicknesses, cross-sectional measurements, and progressive totals — are frequently missing or collapsed into broad figures.
When digital entry of every line-by-line measurement is genuinely difficult or impractical due to technical or operational constraints, the department cannot simply abandon the requirement for detailed verification. Two non-negotiable actions become mandatory:
	1.	Upload the original bill submitted by the contractor in full, as presented.
	2.	Upload a clear, legible PDF of the physical Measurement Book containing the complete line-by-line measurements.
Without these two documents being made publicly accessible (or at least available to audit and vigilance authorities), the electronic abstract remains an opaque summary that cannot be independently verified. An abstract without its underlying detailed measurements is functionally equivalent to an unaudited claim.
Missing Quality Control Test Abstracts
Compounding the measurement problem is the routine absence of Quality Control (QC) test abstracts in the electronic billing trail. Laboratory test results for materials — particularly those related to bituminous works — are either not uploaded or uploaded in incomplete form. This leaves no readily accessible record of whether the materials used met the specified standards for grade, density, stability, or binder content.
Bitumen CRC and Consumption Statements: The Single Largest Corruption Vector
Nowhere is the transparency deficit more dangerous than in the handling of bitumen. Two critical documents are frequently missing from electronic bills:
	•	Copies of the Bitumen CRC (Consignment Receipt Certificate / Material Receipt Certificate)
	•	The Bitumen Consumption Statement correlating the quantity of bitumen actually used with the quantities claimed in the bill
Industry observers and vigilance professionals have long identified the CRC and its verification process as the “single mother of corruption” in PWD road works. The pathway is well-known:
	•	Genuine CRCs can be manipulated or substituted.
	•	Fake or recycled CRCs can be introduced.
	•	Verification of the CRC itself can be compromised through collusion.
	•	The same CRC is used by the same contractor across multiple projects simultaneously — one consignment receipt is recycled to claim bitumen delivery at several work sites, multiplying the fraudulent payment several times over. This is among the hardest frauds to detect without a centralised CRC registry that cross-checks each CRC number against all bills statewide.
	•	Consumption statements can be inflated or disconnected from actual site usage.
When these documents are not uploaded into the electronic billing system, the opportunity for detection collapses. An auditor or supervising officer is left with an abstract figure for “bitumen used” without the documentary chain that would prove (or disprove) the claim. In a sector where bitumen constitutes a major cost component and quality failure leads to rapid pavement deterioration, this omission is not a minor administrative lapse — it is a structural enabler of fraud.
Why This Matters
Road works executed with inflated measurements or sub-standard bitumen do not merely waste public money. They produce roads that fail prematurely, impose higher maintenance costs on the state, and endanger public safety. When the electronic system designed to prevent these outcomes itself becomes a vehicle for concealment, the reform loses credibility.
The Way Forward
If full digital line-by-line entry remains operationally difficult in the short term, the minimum corrective action is clear and immediate:
	•	Mandate simultaneous upload of the contractor’s original bill and the complete physical MB (line-by-line) as PDF attachments to every electronic bill.
	•	Require upload of the QC test abstract, Bitumen CRC copies, and the corresponding bitumen consumption statement as mandatory enclosures.
	•	Treat the absence of these documents as a ground for withholding payment and initiating scrutiny.
Transparency is not achieved by digitising summaries. It is achieved by making the underlying evidence — the detailed measurements, the material certificates, and the consumption records — visible and verifiable. Until the electronic Measurement Book ceases to be a mere abstract and becomes a true digital twin of the physical record, the system will continue to offer convenience to those who wish to obscure rather than illuminate the actual work done.

The Missing Contractor Ledger: 15 Years of Online Payments, Zero Financial Accountability

Online contractor payment has been operational in Rajasthan for 15 years. In that entire period, the government has never built a Contractor Ledger — a per-contractor digital account statement showing every payment received, every deposit held, and every refund due.

This is not an oversight. It is a structural omission that serves one purpose: keeping contractors in the dark about their own money.

EMD, SD, and Dep-V — The Silent Deposits Nobody Returns

Every government contractor deposits three types of security:
  • EMD (Earnest Money Deposit) — paid at tender stage
  • SD (Security Deposit) — deducted from running bills
  • Dep-V (Additional Security / Performance Deposit) — deducted from bills

These deposits are refundable on completion of the contract and after the defect liability period. But without a ledger, the contractor cannot know:
  • How much has been deducted cumulatively
  • When it becomes due for refund
  • Whether it was ever refunded

When a contractor dies, when records are lost, when a firm winds up, or when the track is simply broken due to staff transfer — this money sits silently in the department's accounts. For years. Sometimes forever. The department never volunteers to return it. No notice is issued. No statement is sent.

This is not benign neglect. This is the systematic retention of public-origin private money.

The Mandatory Fix: Contractor Ledger + Annual Statement

The solution is straightforward and non-negotiable:
  1. Every contractor registered with the department must have a digital ledger — auto-maintained by the software — showing all transactions.
  2. On 31st March every year, the system must automatically email each contractor their complete balance statement: payments received, deposits held, amounts due for refund.
  3. Silence must not be an option. The statement must be generated and delivered by default, without the contractor having to ask.

e-Procurement Exists — Why Does e-EMD Refund Not?

The government has a fully operational e-Procurement portal. Tenders are floated online. Bids are submitted online. EMD is collected online through the same portal.

But when it comes to refunding that EMD — the portal has no automated refund system. The contractor who paid online must chase a manual process. This asymmetry is not accidental.

If the government can build a system to collect EMD digitally — it can build a system to refund it digitally. The fact that it has not done so in 15 years is not a technical limitation. It is a policy choice — one that benefits the department at the contractor's expense.

This is not administrative failure alone. It is an institutionalised structure of financial dominance over contractors — a form of silent coercion dressed in the language of process.

The Department\'s Reluctance to Act

While the department seldom withdraws work on account of over-delay, substandard quality, or other contractual breaches, approximately 200 contracts have been rescinded for incomplete execution. In these cases, risk-and-cost tenders have been invited to complete the unfinished work at the defaulting contractor\'s expense.

However, there is no regular, systematic monitoring of recovery proceedings against such defaulters. The financial dues — cost difference between the original contract and the higher risk-and-cost tender, plus damages — are simply not being pursued with any consistency or urgency.

Three Named Examples — Ground-Level Evidence

1. Bhairavji Bridge, Banswara
The contract for Bhairavji Bridge in Banswara district was rescinded after the contractor failed to complete the project within the agreed timeframe and to specified quality. A risk-and-cost tender was subsequently invited. Despite the clear contractual obligation for recovery, the department has not followed through on recovering the dues from the defaulting contractor.

2. Kotada Road, Udaipur
The Kotada Road project in Udaipur district similarly saw its contract rescinded for incomplete execution. Risk-and-cost proceedings were initiated. Yet the defaulting contractor has not been pursued for the differential cost recovery that the department is legally entitled and obligated to claim.

3. Gannon Dunkerlay — Named Contractor, Unrecovered Dues
Gannon Dunkerlay, a contractor of significant standing, had a contract rescinded. The department\'s entitlement to recover substantial dues from this company on account of the incomplete project remains unexercised. No recovery action of note has been documented or pursued.

What This Reveals

The pattern across these three cases — and across the approximately 200 rescinded contracts statewide — is consistent:
  • Rescission is initiated (eventually, and rarely).
  • Risk-and-cost tenders are floated.
  • The additional cost incurred by the department is calculated.
  • And then — nothing. Recovery proceedings stall, are not monitored, and the defaulting contractor walks free of financial consequence.

This is not administrative inefficiency alone. In a system where a contractor faces no financial consequence for abandoning a project, the incentive structure is fundamentally broken. Contractors learn that rescission is a paper threat — not a real financial liability.

The consequences are borne by the public: incomplete infrastructure, higher re-tendering costs, and no accountability for the original failure.

What Must Change
  • A centralised Risk-and-Cost Recovery Register must be maintained and monitored — with project name, defaulting contractor name, amount recoverable, and status of recovery proceedings.
  • Recovery must be treated as a legal and financial priority — not an afterthought.
  • Defaulting contractors must be blacklisted from future tenders until full recovery is made.
  • Named cases — Bhairavji Bridge, Kotada Road, Gannon Dunkerlay — must be subject to immediate CAG scrutiny and ACB investigation where collusion in non-recovery is suspected.
